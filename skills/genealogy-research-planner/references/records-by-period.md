# Records by period: what exists, from when, and what it proves

Genealogy is done with the records a society happened to create. Knowing
what was being written at a place and time tells you where a fact can be
found and, as importantly, where it cannot. France is covered in detail;
the last section gives the equivalent milestones elsewhere.

Before 1792 the unit is the parish; from 1792 the commune, created from the
parish in most cases but not all (mergers, splits, hamlets attached to
another parish). Départements date from 1790; their archives hold the
greffe copies for their current territory, so a commune that changed
département has records in two places.

## Vital events: which record answers which question

| Question | Best record | Good substitute | Weak but useful |
| -------- | ----------- | --------------- | --------------- |
| Birth date and place | Birth act / baptism | Marriage act (copied from birth act after 1804), military register, marginal mentions | Age at death or marriage (rounded), census age |
| Parents of an adult | Their own marriage act (both parents named, alive or dead) | Baptism / birth act, marriage contract, succession table, death act after 1823 | Godparents' names, witnesses, census household |
| Death date and place | Death act / burial | Succession and absence table, marginal mention on birth act (after 1945), "feu" on a child's marriage act (BEF) | Absence from a later census, tombstone |
| Marriage date and place | Marriage act | Marriage contract (enregistrement table), publications, livret de famille | First child's baptism (AFT and BEF bounds), marginal mention on birth act |
| Spouse's identity | Marriage act, marriage contract | Children's acts (mother's maiden name), death act ("époux de", "veuve de") | Census household |
| Sex and given names | Birth act | Baptism | Any later act |

The marriage act is the pivot of French research: after 1804 it copies
the birth data of both spouses, names all four parents with their status
and residence, and lists witnesses with their kinship. Find the marriage
first.

## Relationship-bearing records

Records that state a family link rather than let it be inferred:

- **Marriage acts and contracts**: parents, previous spouses, the notary.
- **Dispenses de consanguinité et d'affinité** (diocesan archives, series G
  or diocesan): the common ancestor spelled out over 2 to 4 generations,
  with a genealogical table. The single most powerful pre-1792 record for
  rural families who intermarried.
- **Successions**: tables de successions et absences, déclarations de
  succession (from 1791), inventaires après décès and partages (notarial):
  heirs by name, residence, kinship and share.
- **Tutelles et curatelles** (justice records, series B before 1790):
  guardians appointed after a parent's death, with relatives assembled
  and their kinship.
- **Military registers**: parents' names and residence.
- **Reconnaissances et légitimations**: paternity of natural children.
- **Testaments** and **donations entre vifs**: kin named.

## Residence, occupation and movement

- Censuses 1836 onward every 5 years (1871 taken in 1872; none in 1916 or
  1941), household by household; occupation, kin position, from 1872 birth
  year, from 1906 birth place.
- Military registers 1867 onward: successive addresses until age 45.
- Passports intérieurs and livrets d'ouvrier (series M): itineraries of
  workers.
- Cadastre: owners of each plot, changes over time (matrices).
- Electoral lists: adult men from 1848 with birth date and address.
- Annuaires and almanachs (Gallica): tradesmen and professionals.
- Notarial leases and sales: farmers and their farms.
- Parish registers' "de la paroisse de" and civil acts' "domicilié à":
  residence at the event.

## Records of the poor, the marginal and the mobile

Enfants assistés / enfants trouvés (hospice registers, series X), hospital
admissions, prison registers (écrou, series Y), police registers of
prostitution and vagrancy, bagne records (ANOM), mendicity depots,
emigration files (passports abroad, series M, consular records at the
Archives diplomatiques), naturalisation dossiers (Archives nationales BB/11
and online index), seamen's registers (inscription maritime, series P at
AD and Service historique de la Défense), colonial état civil (ANOM).

## Gaps and losses

- Registers begin late in many parishes (1650-1700) and have gaps.
- Paris état civil before 1860 burned in 1871; the *reconstitution* covers
  about a third, indexed online. Substitutes: parish registers of
  catholicité, notarial minutes (Minutier central), enregistrement,
  cemetery registers, the 1817-1846 population censuses.
- Communal copies were lost in wars (1870, 1914-1918 in the north and
  east, 1940-1944); the greffe copy at the AD usually survives, and the
  reverse.
- Protestant baptisms and marriages 1685-1787 are largely absent
  (clandestine or in Catholic registers as "nouveaux convertis").
- Before 1737 the greffe copy is often missing; before 1667 there is one
  copy or none.

## Access rules

État civil: births and marriages public 75 years after the act (or 25
years after the person's death), deaths immediately; the archives publish
what is open online. Notarial minutes: 75 years. Judicial and police
files: 75 to 100 years. Censuses: freely available once deposited.
Military registers: fully open after 120 years from birth, with medical
data redacted otherwise. Living persons' data must not be collected
beyond the tree's needs.
