# Poland and Prussia

Ancestors from the lands between the Oder and the Bug lived under four
successive record regimes in two centuries, with borders, languages,
alphabets, place names and sometimes surnames changing under them. The
first task is always to fix the village on a map and decide which state
it belonged to at each date; every other question follows from that.

## Fix the place first

A French record says "né à Posen" or "à Konigsberg, Prusse" or, more
often, gives a misspelled village. Before any register search:

1. Get every spelling from every record (marriage, death, naturalisation
   file, foreigner's registration card, military register).
2. Identify the place with a gazetteer that gives the parish and the
   civil registration office: Meyers Orts- und Verkehrs-Lexikon (1912,
   searchable at meyersgaz.org, with the Standesamt and both confessions'
   parishes), Kartenmeister (kartenmeister.com, for lands east of the
   Oder-Neisse with German and Polish names), Słownik geograficzny
   Królestwa Polskiego (1880-1902, for the Russian and Austrian
   partitions, in Polish), and the Polish TERYT register for current
   names.
3. Record the German name, the Polish name, the Kreis / powiat, the
   Regierungsbezirk / voivodeship at the relevant dates, the Catholic and
   Evangelical parish, and the Standesamt. Villages usually did not have
   their own parish; the records are in a neighbouring town.

## Partitions and regimes

| Territory | Regime and dates | Record regime |
| --------- | ---------------- | ------------- |
| Prussian partition: Posen (Poznań), West Prussia (Gdańsk, Toruń), and old Prussian Silesia, Pomerania, East Prussia | Prussia 1772/1793/1815 to 1918-1920 (Posen, West Prussia to Poland; Silesia, Pomerania, East Prussia to Germany until 1945) | Church registers (Kirchenbücher) as the only record until 1 Oct 1874, then civil Standesamt registers in German alongside; Jewish civil registration from 1812 in Prussia |
| Duchy of Warsaw 1807-1815, then Congress Poland (Russian partition) | Napoleonic Duchy, then Russian rule 1815-1915 | Napoleonic civil registration from 1808 kept by the parish priest as civil officer, in Polish; from 1826 kept per confession (Catholic, Evangelical, Jewish, Orthodox, Mennonite); in Russian (Cyrillic) 1868-1915; Polish again from 1915-1918 |
| Galicia (Austrian partition): Kraków, Lwów, Przemyśl, Tarnów | Austria 1772/1795 to 1918 | Parish registers in Latin, columnar printed forms from 1784, serving as civil records; Jewish registers from 1789 in German |
| Free City of Danzig 1920-1939 | League of Nations | Standesamt continues |
| Interwar Poland 1918-1939 | Second Republic | Standesamt in Polish in the former Prussian lands; confessional registers continue elsewhere until 1946 |
| Post-1945 | Poland gains Silesia, Pomerania, East Prussia's south; German population expelled | Pre-1945 German records partly in Polish state archives, partly evacuated to Germany, partly lost |

## Record types by regime

**Prussian lands**

| Record | Dates | Where | Notes |
| ------ | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Kirchenbücher (Catholic, Evangelical) | 16th-17th c.- | Polish diocesan and state archives, Evangelisches Zentralarchiv Berlin (EZA), Archion, Matricula, FamilySearch | Latin (Catholic) or German (Evangelical); duplicates (Kirchenbuchduplikate) sent to the courts from 1794 survive in state archives |
| Standesamt: Geburten, Heiraten, Sterbefälle | 1 Oct 1874- | Polish state archives (Archiwum Państwowe) for the Polish side, Standesamt I Berlin / Landesarchiv Berlin for evacuated eastern registers, local Standesämter for the German side | Printed German forms; second copy (Nebenregister) at the court; marriage act names parents, birth data, witnesses |
| Prussian censuses | 1867, 1871, 1880… | Mostly destroyed; household lists (Seelenlisten) survive locally | |
| Military: Stammrollen, Verlustlisten 1914-1918 | 19th c., 1914-1918 | Verlustlisten indexed at des.genealogy.net; Prussian army files largely destroyed 1945 | Regimental histories (Ranglisten) for officers |
| Grundakten, Hypothekenbücher | 18th c.- | State archives | Land ownership, inheritances |
| Auswanderungsakten, Passagierlisten | 19th c. | Hamburg passenger lists 1850-1934 (Ancestry / Staatsarchiv Hamburg), Bremen lists mostly lost (1920-1939 partial) | Emigration to the Americas and to the Ruhr |
| Heimatortskarteien, Lastenausgleichsarchiv | after 1945 | Kirchlicher Suchdienst, Bundesarchiv Bayreuth | Expellee families with pre-1945 addresses and composition |

**Congress Poland (Russian partition)**

| Record | Dates | Notes |
| ------ | ----- | ----- |
| Akta stanu cywilnego | 1808- | Napoleonic narrative form: date and hour, declarant with age, occupation and residence, witnesses, child's name, parents; marriage acts give ages, birthplaces, parents, banns, consent; Polish 1808-1867, Russian 1868-1915. Annual alphabetical index (skorowidz) at the end of each year's book |
| Księgi ludności stałej / niestałej | 1810s- | Permanent and non-permanent population books by town: household composition, moves; the equivalent of the Belgian population register, when they survive |
| Spisy parafialne, status animarum | 18th-19th c. | Parish household lists |
| Conscription lists (Russian army) | 1874- | Uneven survival |

**Galicia (Austrian partition)**

Columnar parish books from 1784: births (with house number, legitimacy,
parents, godparents), marriages (ages, house numbers, parents, witnesses),
deaths (cause, house number). House numbers link families across books.
Catholic in Latin, Greek Catholic in Latin or Church Slavonic/Ukrainian,
Jewish in German then Polish. Josephine (1785-1788) and Franciscan
(1819-1820) cadastral surveys list heads of household by house number.

## Languages, scripts and names

- **Scripts**: German records to 1941 are in Kurrent (old German cursive)
  with printed Fraktur headings; Russian records 1868-1915 in Cyrillic
  with Polish names transliterated phonetically (Wojciech → Войцех,
  Grzegorz → Гжегож), and a Polish name form often added in brackets.
  Latin in Catholic books everywhere. Polish orthography for names in
  Polish-language records.
- **Given names** exist in three or four forms: Latin (Adalbertus,
  Hedwigis, Laurentius), Polish (Wojciech, Jadwiga, Wawrzyniec), German
  (Albert, Hedwig, Lorenz), Russian transliteration. Adalbertus is
  Wojciech, not Albert; Hyacinthus is Jacek; Bartholomaeus is Bartłomiej.
  Keep a table for the family.
- **Surnames**: Polish surnames take gender and marital endings
  (Kowalski / Kowalska; Nowak / Nowakowa for the wife, Nowakówna for the
  unmarried daughter, in older records); German scribes wrote them
  phonetically (Szymański → Schimanski, Wróblewski → Wroblewski); Polish
  scribes Polonised German names (Schmidt → Szmit, Müller → Miler). The
  same family can appear as Kowalski, Kowalsky and Kowalskyj. Kashubian,
  Masurian and Silesian regional forms add variants.
- **Place names** changed with each regime (Posen / Poznań, Bromberg /
  Bydgoszcz, Danzig / Gdańsk, Breslau / Wrocław, Lemberg / Lwów / Lviv);
  villages were renamed or Germanised in the 1930s. Record both names and
  the date each was used.

## Access rules

Poland: civil acts pass to the state archives 100 years after the year
of the register for births, 80 for marriages and deaths; before that they
are at the Urząd Stanu Cywilnego and copies are issued to direct
descendants. Germany: Standesamt births 110 years, marriages 80, deaths
30 (Personenstandsgesetz 2009); after that, at the archive, often online.
Church books: by the archive's policy, typically 100-120 years for
baptisms.

## From France back to Poland or Prussia

Three main streams, each with its own paper trail:

1. **The Great Emigration (after 1831) and political exiles**: dossiers of
   Polish refugees at the Archives nationales (F/7 police générale,
   F/15 secours) and departmental series M (dépôts de réfugiés at Avignon,
   Besançon, Bourges, Châteauroux, Le Puy); the *Société historique et
   littéraire polonaise* (Bibliothèque polonaise de Paris) for the
   educated exiles.
2. **Miners and industrial workers 1919-1939**: Poles recruited under the
   1919 Franco-Polish convention to the Nord and Pas-de-Calais coalfields,
   Lorraine steel and iron, Saint-Étienne, Montceau; many came via the
   Ruhr ("Westphaliens", Poles who had first migrated to Westphalia from
   Posen and West Prussia before 1914, then moved to France 1922-1924
   with their German-recorded children). Records: *fiches d'immatriculation*
   and *cartes d'identité d'étranger* (AD series M, 4 M), company personnel
   files (Centre historique minier de Lewarde for the Nord coalfield,
   ANMT Roubaix for company archives), naturalisation dossiers (BB/11, then
   the 1930s dossiers at the Archives nationales, Pierrefitte), the
   Polish consulates' registers (Archiwum Akt Nowych, Warsaw, fonds of the
   consulates in Lille, Paris, Strasbourg, Marseille), Polish parishes in
   France (Polish Catholic Mission, Paris, with baptism and marriage
   registers), Polish schools and associations (Sokół, KSMP), and the
   Ruhr Standesamt and Catholic registers for children born there.
   Repatriation to Poland 1945-1949 is documented in the Archiwum Akt
   Nowych and in French departmental files.
3. **Prussian and German Poles before 1914**: seasonal and industrial
   workers registered as Germans in French records; their German passports
   and the "Prusse" or "Allemagne" birthplace conceal a Polish village.
   The name's Polish form in a Catholic marriage act or a child's baptism
   at a Polish mission is the clue.

For any stream: the French marriage act after 1897 gives the birthplace as
declared, the naturalisation dossier holds the birth certificate sent from
Poland or Germany (with the exact parish or Standesamt), and the
foreigner's identity card file gives the passport data.

## Brick-wall specifics

- **Find the parish, not the village.** Most villages had no church; Meyers
  or Słownik geograficzny gives the parish, the Standesamt and the
  confession.
- **Use the right index for the regime**: Poznań Project and BaSIA for
  Prussian Poland, Geneteka for Congress Poland and beyond, JRI-Poland for
  Jewish lines, Archion for Evangelical Prussians.
- **Read the marriage act, whatever the language**: the Napoleonic form
  names both parents, ages, birthplaces and witnesses; the Prussian
  Standesamt marriage form names parents and birth data. Russian acts have
  a fixed structure that can be read with a template even without the
  language.
- **Confession matters**: a Polish-named family may be Evangelical
  (Masuria, Cieszyn Silesia), a German-named one Catholic (Silesia,
  Ermland). Search both books of the place.
- **Kirchenbuchduplikate** in Polish state archives (from 1794 in Prussia,
  from 1808 in Congress Poland) survive where the parish original burned.
- **House numbers** in Galicia and Seelenlisten in Prussia tie families
  across books; note them.
- **Expulsion and destruction 1939-1945**: check the Landesarchiv Berlin
  index, the Heimatortskarteien and Arolsen before concluding a record is
  lost.
- **Name changes**: French records may carry an administrative
  francisation (Kowalski → Kowalsky or Cowalski; Szczepański → Stefanski)
  or a change by naturalisation decree published in the Journal officiel;
  search the decree.
- **Two families with the same surname in one parish** are the norm in
  Polish villages; distinguish by house number, godparents and the
  mother's maiden name, never by surname and year alone.
