# Brick-wall strategies

What to do when the direct records do not answer. Each strategy says when
it applies, what to do, and what it can prove. Use several together and
log each as its own search.

## First, doubt the wall

Most brick walls are a wrong fact one or two generations downstream: a
birthplace copied from an index, an age taken literally, a same-name
merge, a mother's surname confused with a stepmother's. Before searching
further, re-read every source for the last two proven generations and
check that each link is stated by a record, not inferred. List the
assumptions; test the weakest.

## Name variants and indexes

Surnames were spelled by the scribe, phonetically, in the local dialect.
Before any index search write the variant list: consonant doubling
(Dupont/Duppont), final consonant (Dupont/Dupond/Dupon), particles and
agglutination (Du Pont/Dupont/Dupond), regional phonetics (Bernard/Bernat
in Occitan areas, -eau/-aud/-ault/-ot in the west, Ch-/K- in Breton and
Picard areas), Latinised or Frenchified forms (Joannes/Jean, Guilhem/
Guillaume), given-name substitution (Jean-Baptiste recorded as Baptiste,
Marie-Anne as Anne). Search each variant separately; indexes do not do
phonetic matching reliably. For pre-1792 registers, browse rather than
index: read the years around the expected date page by page.

## The marriage-first rule

Whatever the question, find the person's marriage act (or contract) first.
After 1804 it names parents and birth data; before, it names parents,
parishes of origin and witnesses with kinship. Look in the bride's parish
first (marriages were celebrated there), then the groom's, then the
tables décennales of the canton. If the couple's first child is known,
the marriage is usually within the three years before that baptism.

## Collateral lines

Siblings, uncles and cousins share the ancestors you seek and left their
own records. A brother's marriage act names the same parents; a sister's
death act may give the parents when the target's does not; a cousin's
dispensation of consanguinity draws the whole tree. Reconstitute the
family (all children of the couple, from baptisms and censuses) before
going up a generation; then use whichever child's records are richest.

## Cluster research (FAN)

Friends, associates and neighbours: godparents, marriage witnesses,
declarants, neighbours in the census, co-signers of notarial acts,
employers. Godparents are usually grandparents, uncles and aunts or older
siblings in the 17th-18th centuries; a repeated witness across several
acts is kin. Build the cluster's tree beside the target's; when the target
"appears from nowhere" in a parish, the cluster usually came from the
same place and shows where.

## Bracket the dates

Turn an unknown date into a range with two records: alive at a witness
signature (AFT), dead at a child's marriage where called "feu" (BEF),
first child's baptism gives the marriage's upper bound, a census gives an
age. Record the range in the tree (`BET … AND …`) and search only inside
it. A bracketed death is found in the tables de successions et absences
faster than in year-by-year registers.

## Widen the geography deliberately

Rings: same parish other years; adjacent parishes (people married across
the boundary and baptised at the nearest church, not the legal one); the
market town; the spouse's parish; the seasonal migration destinations of
the region (masons of the Creuse to Paris, Savoyards to Lyon, Auvergnats
to Spain); the town where the trade was learned. Use the diocesan
boundaries before 1792 for dispensations and the recruitment office
boundaries for military registers.

## Substitute records for lost registers

| Lost | Substitute |
| ---- | ---------- |
| Parish register years missing | Greffe copy at the AD; diocesan dispensations; notarial contracts; tutelles; relevés by associations made before loss |
| Civil register (commune copy) destroyed | Greffe copy at the AD (or the reverse); tables décennales; registres de catholicité |
| Paris before 1860 | Reconstitution fichier; parish catholicité; Minutier central; enregistrement; cemetery registers |
| Marriage act not found | Marriage contract via enregistrement tables; publications in the other commune; livret de famille; children's acts' "mariés à" mentions |
| Death act not found | Successions et absences tables; marginal mention on the birth act after 1945; "veuve" on a later act; cemetery; hospital registers |
| Birth act not found | Baptism (catholicité), military register, marriage act copy, passport, naturalisation dossier |

## Illegitimacy, foundlings and recognitions

A child "de père inconnu" may be recognised later (marginal mention from
1917, or a recognition act in the register, or legitimation at the
parents' marriage where the act says "ont déclaré reconnaître"). Search
the mother's later marriage; the child often takes the husband's name.
Foundlings: hospice registers (enfants assistés, series X) with the
admission note, the *bulletin* of abandonment and the wet-nurse
placements; the surname was often invented by the hospice. Godparents of
a natural child are frequently the mother's kin.

## Remarriages and blended households

A widow remarried within a year or two; children appear in the census
under the stepfather's household with their own surname or the
stepfather's. Check the tables décennales for a second marriage of either
parent; read the marriage act for the children legitimised or the
"veuve de" clause; look for tutelle records naming a guardian for minor
children. Two families with the same father and different mothers are
often merged into one by mistake.

## Same-name candidates

List every same-name person in the parish and its neighbours for the
possible birth range. For each, find what excludes them: a burial in
infancy, a marriage elsewhere, a different occupation or residence in the
census, an age incompatible with a later act, a different cluster of
godparents and witnesses, a different signature. Keep the exclusion
table in the log; it is evidence for the identification of the survivor.
The infant-death-then-namesake pattern (a child dies, the next of the same
sex gets the name) means two individuals.

## Migration and mobility

Follow occupation and marriage: journaliers and domestics moved parish to
parish; artisans followed apprenticeship towns; soldiers married where
garrisoned. Sources: military registers' address list (1867+), passports
intérieurs, livrets d'ouvrier, censuses giving birth place (1906+),
marriage acts in the destination naming the origin parish, the
enregistrement of the destination town. For emigrants: passenger lists
(Le Havre, Bordeaux, Marseille departures; Ellis Island, Canadian and
Argentine arrivals), consular état civil, naturalisation files abroad.

## Timelines and negative evidence

Write the person's life as a dated timeline including every record
searched and not found. Gaps show where a record should exist ("no
baptism in the parish 1793-1797 although the family is present in 1792
and 1798" points to a temporary move or a lost register). Negative
searches with precise scope are evidence: they exclude candidates and
places. Log them with the exact registers and years covered.

## Knowing when to stop

A question is closed when a record states the answer and the alternatives
are excluded, or when the records that could answer it are shown not to
exist (lost, never created, or closed by access rules). Say which, cite
the searches, mark the question abandoned with its reason, and record the
one event that would reopen it (a new online index, an association's
relevé, a notary's minutes deposited). Endless searching without a
framed question is not research.
