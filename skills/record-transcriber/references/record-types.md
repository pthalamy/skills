# Record types: what each contains and what it proves

Use this to know what to look for in an act and to grade what it proves.
"Primary" and "secondary" refer to the information's origin (first-hand
and contemporaneous, or reported), the distinction the gedcom-maintainer
skill uses to set `QUAY`. Dates are for France; neighbouring countries
follow similar patterns with their own milestones.

## Parish registers (before 1792)

Milestones: 1539 (Villers-Cotterêts, baptisms in French), 1579 (Blois,
marriages and burials), 1667 (Saint-Germain-en-Laye, duplicate register),
1736 (both copies signed, formulas standardised). Before 1667 a parish
often has one copy with gaps; after 1736 the *collection communale* and
the *collection du greffe* both exist and may differ in detail.

| Act | Normally contains | Primary for | Secondary or absent |
| --- | ----------------- | ----------- | ------------------- |
| Baptism | Date of baptism, often birth date, child's given name(s), parents (father's occupation, mother's maiden name), parish or hamlet, godparents with kinship and signatures | Baptism date, parents' names, godparents | Birth date if not stated ("né le même jour" is common but not universal) |
| Marriage | Date, banns and dispensations, spouses with age or majority, parish of origin, parents (living or dead), previous spouses, consent, witnesses with kinship | Marriage date and place, spouses' identities, witnesses | Ages (rounded), parents' status, spouses' birthplaces |
| Burial | Date of burial, name, age, sometimes death date, spouse or parents, witnesses | Burial date | Age (often rounded to 5 or 10), death date if only "hier", parentage of adults |

Baptisms usually omit the mother's surname before the 17th century, and
burials of women may name only the husband. Protestant registers (before
1685 and 1787-1792), Jewish registers and *registres des abjurations*
follow their own forms.

## Civil registration (from 1792)

Held by the commune, in duplicate (mairie and greffe, now archives
départementales). Free-form in years I to XII, structured after the Code
civil (1804), with printed forms increasingly from the 1820s.

| Act | Normally contains | Primary for | Secondary |
| --- | ----------------- | ----------- | --------- |
| Naissance | Date and hour of birth, place (house), sex, given names, father (age, occupation, residence) usually declaring, mother (name, age, occupation), two witnesses with ages, occupations, residences, signatures | Birth date, place, parents' names | Parents' ages, mother's birthplace (rare before 1900) |
| Mariage | Publications, spouses with birth date and place (from produced acts), occupation, residence, parents present or deceased with death date and place, previous spouses, marriage contract, legitimated children, four witnesses with kinship | Marriage date and place, spouses' identities, consent | Spouses' birth data (copied from acts, usually reliable), parents' deaths (copied), ages of witnesses |
| Décès | Date and hour of death, place, name, age, birth place and date, occupation, residence, spouse, parents (living or dead), declarants with relationship | Death date and place | Everything about the deceased's origins; depends entirely on the informant's knowledge |

Milestones: 1793 tables décennales; 1823 parents of the deceased more
regularly stated; 1850 mention of a marriage contract compulsory; 1897
marginal mentions of marriage and divorce; 1917 recognition and
legitimation; 1919 one declarant suffices for a death; 1945 marginal
mention of death on the birth act; 1955 births registered where they
occurred, with the mother's maiden name on the child's act throughout.
Access: births and marriages are public after 75 years, deaths
immediately; anything more recent needs the person's authorisation.

A birth act's marginal mentions can carry a whole life: marriage(s) with
spouse's name and place, divorce, death. Read them.

## Tables décennales and indexes

Ten-year alphabetical tables per commune and act type, from 1793. They
give name, date and act number and nothing else. Derivative: use them to
find the act, cite the act. Online indexes (Geneanet, FamilySearch,
Filae, departmental archives' name search) are derivatives too, with
transcription error rates high enough that a date from an index alone
is `QUAY 1`.

## Census returns

*Listes nominatives* from 1836 every five years (1841, 1846… with 1871
moved to 1872, none in 1916 and 1941), by commune, street or hamlet,
household by household: name, given name, age or birth year (from 1872
birth year, from 1906 birth year and place), occupation, position in the
household (chef, épouse, fils, domestique, pensionnaire), nationality
(from 1851), employer (from 1906). Primary for residence and household
composition on census day; ages and birthplaces are as reported by
whoever answered and drift by several years between censuses. Two
same-name people in one commune are told apart by household.

## Military registers

*Registres matricules* from 1867 (class of 1867 onward), by recruitment
office: class (year the man turned 20), matricule number, full name,
birth date and place, residence, occupation, parents' names and
residence, physical description (height, hair, eyes, face, marks),
education level, campaigns, wounds, decorations, successive addresses
until age 45. Primary for description and service; birth data copied from
the birth act and therefore reliable; the address list is the best record
of migration for men of those generations. Earlier: *contrôles de
troupes* (18th century), conscription lists (from 1798), *tirage au sort*
tables (1818-1905).

## Notarial records

Marriage contracts (name the parties, parents, dowry and property, often
the residences and occupations of both families and witnesses),
inventories after death (household composition, debts, papers), wills,
partitions, sales, leases. Indexed by *répertoire* of each notary and,
from 1693, by the *contrôle des actes* and, from 1791, the *enregistrement*
(tables des contrats de mariage, des successions et absences). Primary
for the transaction; kinship stated by parties describing themselves is
usually reliable for close relations. A marriage contract dated shortly
before a marriage act corroborates the marriage and often gives parents
missing from the church act.

## Other frequent records

- *Registres de catholicité* (Catholic registers after 1792): duplicate
  of the religious event, useful when civil acts are lost.
- *Successions et absences* (enregistrement tables, from 1791): death date,
  heirs and their residences.
- *Hypothèques* (from 1799): property transfers by owner name.
- *Passeports intérieurs* and *livrets d'ouvrier* (19th century): physical
  description, origin, itinerary.
- *Listes électorales* (from 1848): residence and birth date of men.
- *Dispenses de consanguinité* (diocesan archives): family relationships
  spelled out across generations.
- *Registres d'écrou*, *hospices*, *enfants assistés*: identities and
  origins of people who leave few other traces.
- Cemetery concessions and gravestones: death dates (derivative when the
  stone was placed later), family groupings.

## Outside France

Belgium, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, the Rhineland and northern Italy
adopted French civil registration under Napoleon (1796-1811) and kept its
structure. Quebec parish registers follow the French model with the
*greffe* duplicate. Spain and Italy have parish books (*libros
sacramentales*, *stati delle anime* / *status animarum* household
listings) and civil registration from 1871 and 1866 respectively. England
and Wales: parish registers from 1538, printed baptism and burial forms
from 1813, printed marriage forms from 1754, civil registration from 1837
(indexes are derivative, the certificate is the record). German lands:
*Kirchenbücher*, civil *Standesamt* registers from 1874 (1792-1814 in
areas under French rule). United States: town and county vital records
vary by state; church records and censuses (federal every ten years from
1790, names of all household members from 1850) carry the weight.
